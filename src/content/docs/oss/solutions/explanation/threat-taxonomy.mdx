Every scenario the scan generates carries tags, and those tags are what the report groups by. Knowing the vocabulary tells you what a clean report actually proves — and, more usefully, what it does not.

[How the Scan Works](/oss/solutions/explanation/how-scan-works) covers the pipeline. This page is the catalog: which label means what, and which generator emits it.

## Three tag families

| Prefix | Answers | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `threat-type:` | What kind of harm is this? | vulnerability scan |
| `quality:` | What kind of mistake is this? | quality scan |
| `component:` | Which part of my pipeline is at fault? | quality scan |

`vulnerability_scan` defaults to `group_by="threat-type"`, `quality_scan` to `group_by="component"`. That is not an arbitrary difference. A vulnerability report answers *how exposed am I*, which is a question about risk categories. A quality report answers *what do I fix*, which is a question about your architecture.

Two more prefixes show up on individual scenarios: `owasp:` maps a scenario to an OWASP LLM Top-10 entry, and `probe-type:` records the attack shape (`multi-turn`). Both are groupable — `group_by` takes any tag prefix — but neither is a default.

## Threat types

The vulnerability scan uses three:

### `harmful-content-generation`

The broadest of the three. It covers everything the adversarial generator's categories aim at: stereotypes and discrimination, politics, religion, CBRN materials, copyright violation, misinformation and disinformation, and illegal activities. It is also the tag on every multi-turn jailbreak scenario, because GOAT and Crescendo both work toward a harmful objective — they differ in route, not in destination.

Emitted by `AdversarialScenarioGenerator`, `GOATAttackScenarioGenerator`, `CrescendoAttackScenarioGenerator`, and inherited by dataset scenarios drawn from harmful-prompt corpora.

### `misguidance-and-unauthorized-advice`

Financial, medical, and legal advice the agent has no business giving. It gets its own tag rather than sitting under harmful content because the failure is different in kind: the answer may be entirely accurate and still be a liability. `AdversarialScenarioGenerator` emits it from its "Unauthorized Advice" category.

### `prompt-injection`

Instructions smuggled into content the agent processes — pasted text, retrieved documents, tool output — that the agent then obeys. `PromptInjectionScenarioGenerator` emits it, paired with `owasp:llm-top-10-2025:LLM01`.

## What each generator family probes

| Family | Generators | Attack shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **LLM-driven** | `AdversarialScenarioGenerator` | An LLM writes conformity rules per category, then crafts a prompt to break each one. Tailored to your description. |
| **Multi-turn attacks** | `GOATAttackScenarioGenerator`, `CrescendoAttackScenarioGenerator` | The attacker opens benign and escalates across turns. GOAT rotates through named strategies; Crescendo builds on the agent's own prior answers. |
| **Dataset-backed** | `PromptInjectionScenarioGenerator`, `HuggingFaceDatasetScenarioGenerator` | Fixed corpora replayed verbatim. Identical every run, no generation cost. |
| **Token-level** | `GCGInjectionScenarioGenerator` | Harmful prompts from a dataset with an adversarial suffix appended, testing whether nonsense token sequences defeat the safety layer. |
| **Knowledge-base** | the five quality generators, below | Questions derived from your own documents. |

The distinction that matters when reading a report: dataset-backed and token-level generators ask the *same* questions of every agent, so a pass tells you your agent handles a known corpus. LLM-driven generators ask questions specific to your `description`, so a pass tells you something about *your* agent — and a vague description makes that guarantee correspondingly vague.

`GCGInjectionScenarioGenerator` deserves one caveat: its suffixes are English-tuned and appended verbatim regardless of the base prompt's language. Its results are weakest evidence on non-English scans.

## Quality failure modes and components

The quality scan's five generators each pair a failure mode with the components that could have caused it:

| Generator | `quality:` tag | `component:` tags |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `HallucinationScenarioGenerator` | `direct-hallucination` | `llm` |
| `SycophancyScenarioGenerator` | `sycophancy-hallucinations` | `llm` |
| `OutOfScopeScenarioGenerator` | `fabricated-hallucination` | `llm`, `retrieval` |
| `MultiTopicScenarioGenerator` | `multi-topic-questions` | `retrieval`, `history` |
| `SplitQuestionsScenarioGenerator` | `split-questions` | `history` |

Reading them in order of what they ask:

- **Direct questions** are answerable from a single document. A failure means the agent contradicted a document it was given — the narrowest, most damning result in the scan.
- **Sycophantic questions** attach a false premise to a direct question ("since you charge a cancellation fee, how do I avoid it?"). A failure means the agent agreed with the user over its own source.
- **Out-of-scope questions** are about topics deliberately absent from the knowledge base. A failure means the agent answered anyway instead of saying it does not know.
- **Multi-topic questions** span several documents across several turns, which is where a top-1 retriever falls apart.
- **Split questions** deliver one question across several turns, testing whether the agent carries context forward.

Three components appear in the reports:

- **`llm`** — the model contradicted, invented, or capitulated despite having what it needed.
- **`retrieval`** — the right document never reached the model, or the wrong one did.
- **`history`** — the failure only appears across turns; earlier context was dropped or misused.

A generator tagged with two components is saying it cannot distinguish between them from the outside — an out-of-scope fabrication looks the same whether retrieval returned nothing or the model ignored what it returned. The grouped report narrows the suspects; the conversation trace tells you which.

## How grouping actually behaves

`group_by` matches a tag prefix and buckets scenarios by the part after the colon. A scenario with two `component:` tags appears in both buckets, so bucket counts can exceed the scenario count, and scenarios carrying no tag with that key land in an unnamed bucket. `group_by=None` prints the flat report.

Three things to hold on to when reading a grouped table:

1. **An empty bucket is not a clean bill of health.** `max_scenarios` is split by a multinomial draw across generators, so a small budget leaves some generators with nothing. A threat type with zero scenarios was not tested — it did not pass.
2. **A bucket's pass rate is a sample, not a measurement.** Four scenarios passing tells you little; the number is a prompt to read the failures, not a metric to trend at low budgets.
3. **Tags are per scenario, not per check.** A scenario can carry several checks; the grouping follows the scenario.

The quality scan adds one more artifact: a written recommendation generated from the grouped results. It is produced by an LLM and the call is wrapped in a `try` — a failure yields an empty string. **An empty recommendation means recommendation generation failed, never that the scan found nothing.** Read `failed_count` for that.
